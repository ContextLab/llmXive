## Unresolved concerns

- The plan proposes a 'paired t-test' on the difference ($\Delta$ES) between pre-print and journal effect sizes. However, FR-002 and the research context explicitly state that effect sizes are often interval-censored (e.g., 'p < 0.05' implies a range, and effect sizes may be reported as ranges or with missing bounds). A standard paired t-test assumes exact point estimates. Applying a t-test to interval-censored data without a specific censored regression model (like Tobit, which is mentioned as an alternative but not the primary plan) violates construct validity and will produce biased estimates of the mean difference.
- The plan states: 'Exclude pairs where N changes by > 20%... to avoid confounding.' This exclusion criterion introduces selection bias. If peer review systematically leads to larger sample sizes (e.g., by requiring power analysis), excluding these pairs removes the very cases where the 'correction' might be most evident, potentially biasing the estimated $\Delta$ES toward zero. The methodology should model N as a covariate or use a method robust to sample size variation rather than discarding data.
- The plan claims the sample size (N=1000) is 'sufficient to detect moderate effect sizes ($\Delta$ES > 0.3) with 80% power.' This is a power analysis assertion, but the document provides no calculation, effect size distribution assumptions, or variance estimates to support it. Without a documented power calculation, the claim that the sample is sufficient is unsubstantiated, risking a Type II error (false negative) if the true effect is smaller or variance is higher.
- The plan proposes 'P-Curve Analysis' on pre-print vs. journal p-values. P-curve analysis assumes that the p-values are independent and that the underlying effect size is constant across studies. However, since this is a paired design (same study, two versions), the p-values are not independent samples from a population; they are repeated measures. Standard p-curve software (e.g., p-curve app) is designed for meta-analysis of independent studies. Applying it to paired data without adjusting for the dependency structure invalidates the p-curve's estimation of evidential value.
- The matching process relies on 'fuzzy title/author similarity (threshold ≥ 0.8)'. In a field like Quantitative Biology, titles can be very similar for distinct studies. Relying solely on fuzzy matching without a secondary verification step (e.g., checking DOI cross-references in OpenAlex or matching specific statistical claims) risks high false-positive match rates. A false match (pairing Study A pre-print with Study B journal) would introduce massive noise, rendering the $\Delta$ES analysis meaningless.
- FR-002 mandates 'Tobit regression or survival analysis for paired data' for censored effect sizes. The plan.md 'Technical Context' and 'Project Structure' sections list 'effect_size.py' but do not explicitly mention Tobit regression, survival analysis, or the specific handling of censored data in the implementation strategy. The plan only mentions 'paired t-tests' in the summary. This creates a gap where the implementer may not know to implement the required censored-data method.
- FR-004 requires 'separate p-curve analysis' and comparing 'results (e.g., estimated power or p-hacking prevalence)'. The plan.md summary mentions 'p-curve analysis' but does not specify that the implementation must calculate and compare 'estimated power' or 'p-hacking prevalence' specifically, nor does it detail the 'separate' nature of the analysis in the 'Project Structure' or 'Methodology' sections. The plan risks implementing a simple density plot rather than the required p-curve inference.
- FR-005 requires a 'sensitivity analysis sweeping the significance threshold' and reporting the 'significance flip rate'. The plan.md mentions 'sensitivity sweeps' in the summary and 'sensitivity.py' in the structure, but does not explicitly define the 'significance flip rate' metric or the requirement to sweep across 'conventional levels' in the implementation details. The plan lacks the specific definition of the output metric required by the spec.
- SC-002 requires the distribution shift to be measured against a 'null distribution generated via permutation or bootstrapping'. The plan.md 'Technical Context' and 'Methodology' sections do not mention permutation testing, bootstrapping, or the generation of a null distribution for the density ratio. The plan appears to focus on point estimates without the required statistical validation against a null.
- SC-004 requires the sensitivity analysis to verify that the 'direction of the bias... remains consistent across all thresholds'. The plan.md 'sensitivity.py' entry and summary do not explicitly state that the implementation must check for consistency of direction across thresholds, only that it performs a sweep. The specific success criterion of 'consistency' is not backed by a plan element.

While the plan mentions 'sensitivity analysis' and 'effect size', it does not explicitly list the 'N increase flag' logic or the 20% threshold in the 'Project Structure' or 'Methodology' sections. Relying on 'Direct download' for a large-scale pipeline (N=1000) will fail due to paywalls, CAPTCHAs, or dynamic URL generation, making the extraction step infeasible without a specific, accessible source strategy (e.g., Unpaywall, CORE, or specific open-access subsets).

Network latency and PDF parsing overhead will likely exceed the time limit. The plan must account for parallelization (within rate limits) or a significantly smaller sample size, or acknowledge that the 6-hour target is unachievable with sequential I/O.

- The plan proposes comparing p-curve results (estimated power/p-hacking prevalence) between pre-prints and journals. However, p-curve analysis is designed to detect p-hacking in a *single* set of studies by examining the right-skew of significant p-values. Comparing two p-curves derived from the *same* underlying studies (pre-print vs. journal) where the journal version is a revision of the pre-print creates a circular validation: the 'outcome' (journal p-curve) is a deterministic function of the 'predictor' (pre-print p-curve) plus a revision process. The analysis risks conflating 'revision' with 'bias correction' without an independent ground truth for the 'true' effect size. The plan must define an independent validation target (e.g., meta-analytic consensus or replication studies) to verify if the journal version is actually less biased, rather than just different.
- The plan states: 'Exclude pairs where N changes by > 20% or p-values are identical (within rounding error) to avoid confounding and tautological results.' Excluding pairs where p-values are identical is methodologically unsound for detecting bias. If peer review fails to correct bias, p-values *should* remain identical. Excluding these cases creates a selection bias that guarantees a finding of 'change' (bias correction) by definition, rendering the analysis tautological. The analysis must include identical pairs to measure the *rate* of correction, not just the magnitude of change in non-identical pairs.
- The plan proposes a paired t-test on $\Delta$ES (Effect Size Difference). However, effect sizes (e.g., Cohen's d) are non-linear functions of sample size (N) and standard deviation. If N changes between pre-print and journal (even <20%), the standard error of the effect size changes, violating the assumption of equal variance in a standard paired t-test. The plan mentions Tobit regression for censored data but does not specify a method to account for the heteroscedasticity introduced by changing N in the paired difference test. A simple paired t-test on raw $\Delta$ES may yield spurious significance due to variance instability rather than true bias correction.
- The plan treats 'p < 0.05' as interval-censored data [0, 0.05] for general reporting but excludes it from p-curve analysis. This is inconsistent. P-curve analysis specifically relies on the distribution of *significant* p-values. If a large portion of significant results are inequalities (common in pre-prints), excluding them from p-curve analysis introduces massive selection bias, as the excluded set is precisely the set most likely to be p-hacked. The plan must define a statistical method to incorporate interval-censored p-values into the p-curve estimation (e.g., using survival analysis techniques for p-curve) rather than discarding them, or the p-curve results will be invalid.
- The plan defines the 'significance flip rate' as a metric for sensitivity. However, if the pre-print and journal versions report the *same* p-value (e.g., 0.049), the flip rate is zero. If the journal version changes the p-value to 0.051, the flip rate is 1. This metric is highly sensitive to rounding and reporting precision rather than substantive bias. The plan does not account for the fact that many journals require reporting p-values to 3 decimal places, while pre-prints might report 2. This 'flip' may be an artifact of reporting conventions, not statistical bias correction. The analysis needs a robustness check for reporting precision artifacts.
