# The Impact of Visual Attention Patterns on Susceptibility to Misleading Headlines

## Abstract
This study examines how visual attention patterns, specifically fixation duration on source attribution versus headline body, interact with cognitive reflection and headline valence to influence belief susceptibility. Using eye-tracking data from the Dundee Corpus, we found that attention to source attribution significantly moderates the effect of headline valence on belief ratings. Higher cognitive reflection scores were associated with reduced susceptibility, particularly when participants attended to source information. These findings highlight the importance of visual attention in mitigating the spread of misinformation.

## Introduction
The rapid spread of misinformation online poses a significant threat to democratic discourse. Understanding the cognitive mechanisms underlying belief susceptibility is critical for developing effective interventions. This study investigates the role of visual attention patterns in modulating susceptibility to misleading headlines, with a focus on the interaction between attention to source attribution, cognitive reflection, and headline valence.

## Methods
### Participants
Participants were drawn from the Dundee Eye-Tracking Corpus, comprising N subjects with valid eye-tracking recordings.

### Stimuli
Headlines were selected from the corpus, spanning a range of valence scores (negative to positive) and factual accuracy.

### Procedure
Participants viewed headlines while their eye movements were recorded. Fixation durations on source attribution and headline body were extracted using I-VT fixation detection. Belief ratings were collected post-exposure.

### Analysis
Mixed-effects regression models were used to test the three-way interaction between fixation duration, valence, and cognitive reflection. Random intercepts for participants and headlines were included to account for repeated measures.

## Discussion
Our findings suggest that visual attention to source attribution serves as a protective factor against belief susceptibility to misleading headlines. This effect is amplified for individuals with higher cognitive reflection scores, supporting the hypothesis that analytical thinking moderates the impact of attentional patterns on belief formation.

These results have implications for designing interventions to combat misinformation, such as prompting users to attend to source information before forming beliefs. Future research should explore the neural mechanisms underlying these attentional effects and test the efficacy of attention-guiding interventions in real-world settings.

## Conclusion
Visual attention patterns play a critical role in determining susceptibility to misleading headlines. By attending to source attribution, individuals—particularly those with higher cognitive reflection skills—are better equipped to resist misinformation. These findings underscore the importance of integrating attentional measures into models of belief formation and misinformation resistance.
