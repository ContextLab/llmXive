## Research-question validation

### Phenomenon-vs-method check

**Verdict**: pass

The question investigates a substantive clinical phenomenon: the comparative efficacy of a virtual delivery modality versus a traditional in-person modality for treating anxiety. It does not hinge on the performance of a specific machine learning algorithm, computational budget, or software implementation detail, but rather on the real-world therapeutic outcomes observed across human subjects in randomized trials.

### Circularity check

**Verdict**: pass

The predictor variables (anxiety subtype, intervention dose, therapist guidance) are distinct clinical and procedural characteristics of the trials, while the outcome variable (anxiety-symptom severity reduction) is a measured clinical endpoint derived from validated psychometric scales (e.g., STAI, BAI). These data sources are independent; the outcome is not mechanically derived from the predictor variables by construction.

### Triviality check

**Verdict**: pass

Both potential outcomes are scientifically valuable. A finding of non-inferiority would support the scalable adoption of VRET to address therapist shortages, while a finding of significant inferiority would highlight specific limitations of virtual immersion in therapeutic contexts. Furthermore, identifying which subtypes or dosage levels moderate the effect provides actionable clinical guidance regardless of the direction of the main effect.

### Question-narrowing check

**Verdict**: pass

The question explicitly names relationships within the clinical domain (VRET vs. in-person efficacy across anxiety subtypes) rather than focusing on implementation constraints like hardware speed or code efficiency. It asks "how does X behave under Y conditions," which is a valid domain inquiry for a meta-analysis.

### Overall verdict

**Verdict**: validated

All checks pass; the research question targets a genuine, non-circular, and non-trivial gap in clinical psychology literature regarding the comparative effectiveness of virtual versus traditional exposure therapy. The framing is appropriate for a meta-analysis and does not conflate the research goal with implementation details.
