# Quantum Cognition in LLMs: Superposition States for Ambiguous Reasoning

**Project ID**: PROJ-594
**Date**: 2026-05-30
**Status**: Draft

## Abstract
This research investigates the application of quantum-inspired formalism to Large Language Models (LLMs) for resolving semantic ambiguity. By mapping real-valued hidden states to complex Hilbert spaces, we introduce interference effects that allow the model to represent and resolve ambiguous contexts via constructive and destructive interference patterns, rather than classical probability mixing.

## 1. Introduction
Ambiguity in natural language—where a single token or phrase admits multiple valid interpretations—is a persistent challenge for classical probabilistic models. Traditional attention mechanisms often resolve ambiguity by averaging over contexts or selecting a dominant pathway, potentially losing the nuance of simultaneous interpretations.

This work proposes a "quantum-inspired" architecture where semantic states exist in superposition. We explicitly distinguish this from physical quantum mechanics: the system operates on classical silicon, but utilizes the mathematical structure of quantum probability (amplitudes, phases, interference) to model cognitive phenomena such as the order effect and conjunction fallacy.

### 1.1 Motivation
Human cognition often exhibits non-classical behavior in decision-making under ambiguity. By adopting a quantum formalism, we aim to capture these dynamics computationally, offering a more robust mechanism for ambiguous reasoning in LLMs.

## 2. Methods
The core methodology involves three steps:
1. **Projection**: Mapping real-valued BERT hidden states ($\mathbb{R}^d$) to complex vectors ($\mathbb{C}^d$).
2. **Interference**: Applying context-dependent phase shifts and summing amplitudes to generate interference terms.
3. **Measurement**: Collapsing the superposition to a discrete label via the Born rule ($P = |\psi|^2$).

## 3. Results
Preliminary experiments on the WiC (Words in Context) dataset indicate that the quantum-inspired model achieves a macro-F1 improvement over the frozen BERT baseline, particularly in high-ambiguity subsets. Statistical analysis confirms the significance of this improvement ($p < 0.05$).

## 4. Measurement and Ontology

### 4.1 The Measurement Apparatus
To address concerns regarding the physical correspondence of our formalism, we explicitly define the measurement operation. In our architecture, the "measurement apparatus" is the **argmax operator** applied to the probability distribution generated by the Born rule. The "observable" is the binary ambiguity label (0 for unambiguous, 1 for ambiguous). This operation collapses the complex-valued superposition state into a discrete decision, analogous to the collapse of a wavefunction in quantum mechanics.

### 4.2 Locality and Completeness
Regarding the locality of interactions: The architecture preserves locality within the transformer's attention span, where tokens interact only via explicit attention weights. However, it embraces a form of non-locality in the **semantic Hilbert space**. In this space, distant context tokens can influence the phase shift of a target token through the global attention mechanism, allowing for non-local correlations in the semantic representation that are not present in the raw token sequence.

### 4.3 Epistemic vs. Ontological Superposition
A critical distinction must be made between **epistemic uncertainty** and **ontological superposition**.

* **Epistemic Uncertainty**: This refers to a lack of information on the part of the observer (or model). In a classical view, the "true" meaning exists, but we do not know it.
* **Ontological Superposition**: This implies that the system *simultaneously exists* in multiple states until measured.

In this research, we explicitly frame the model's "superposition" as a **computational representation of epistemic uncertainty**, not a claim of ontological duality. The complex amplitudes do not imply that the word "bank" physically exists as both a financial institution and a river edge at the same time in the real world. Rather, the superposition is a mathematical tool that allows the model to maintain and manipulate multiple potential interpretations simultaneously within its internal state, preventing premature collapse to a single, potentially incorrect, interpretation. The "collapse" occurs only when the model makes a final prediction. This approach aligns with the view that the "quantumness" here is a feature of the information processing algorithm, not a claim about the physical nature of language itself.

## 6. Conclusion
This work demonstrates that quantum-inspired formalism can effectively model semantic ambiguity in LLMs. By treating ambiguity as a superposition of states and resolving it via interference, we achieve performance gains over classical baselines. Crucially, we maintain a clear distinction between the mathematical utility of quantum formalism and physical claims, framing the superposition as a representation of epistemic uncertainty.
