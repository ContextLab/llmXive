## Unresolved concerns

- The inclusion criteria allow *quasi‑experimental* designs with a concurrent control group alongside RCTs. Treating these designs identically in the meta‑analysis can compromise causal validity because quasi‑experimental studies are more susceptible to selection bias. Recommend either restricting to RCTs or modelling study design as a moderator / performing sensitivity analyses.
- Multi‑arm studies are handled by *proportional control splitting* (FR‑008). This approach creates artificially correlated effect sizes and can bias variance estimates. Consider using a multivariate meta‑analysis or combining arms according to Cochrane guidance rather than splitting controls.
- Power calculations are provided only for the overall pooled effect (detectable g ≈ 0.45). No power assessment is given for the four planned subgroup families, which may be severely under‑powered and increase false‑negative risk. Include subgroup‑specific power estimates or limit the number of subgroups.
- Component and delivery‑format tags are extracted via regex patterns and, when full text is unavailable, fallback to abstract‑only extraction. Abstracts often lack detailed methodological descriptions, risking misclassification and threatening construct validity. Recommend flagging such records for manual verification or excluding them from subgroup analyses.
- The plan does not incorporate any assessment of individual study risk of bias or quality (e.g., Cochrane RoB2). Ignoring study quality can confound the pooled estimate, especially when low‑quality studies differ systematically in effect size. Add a risk‑of‑bias extraction step and consider weighting or sensitivity analyses based on quality.
- Random‑effects are estimated using the DerSimonian‑Laird method. This estimator is known to underestimate between‑study variance when the number of studies is small, potentially inflating type I error. Consider using a more robust estimator (e.g., REML or Hartung‑Knapp) especially for subgroups with few studies.
- The plan cites ClinicalTrials.gov and OSF as the sole data sources (see the ‘Dataset Strategy’ table). These registries often do not publish the detailed summary statistics (means, SDs) required for Hedges’ g calculation. Relying exclusively on them may leave the pipeline without sufficient numeric outcome data, jeopardizing the ability to compute effect sizes and answer the research question. Consider supplementing registry data with full-text articles (e.g., PubMed‑indexed publications) or a manual data‑extraction step to ensure the necessary statistics are available.
- Principle II requires verification of both registry URLs **and DOIs**, but the plan only logs registry query strings and timestamps; there is no step that validates DOI correctness or existence.
- The Study entity marks `social_skill_domain` as a required field, yet the `cleaned_study.schema.yaml` does not list it in `required`. This creates a mismatch between the data model and its JSON‑Schema contract.
- The description of `effect_sizes.py` mentions computing Hedges’ g and CI but does not state that `n_treatment` and `n_control` are retained in the output CSV, which are required by the `EffectSize` entity and its schema.
- The plan computes Hedges’ g using only post‑intervention means, SDs, and sample sizes (FR‑004, FR‑013). Relying solely on post‑scores ignores baseline measurements, which can bias effect‑size estimates if randomization does not perfectly balance groups. In psychological meta‑analyses it is standard to use change‑score data (pre‑post differences) or, when unavailable, to adjust for baseline imbalance. The current approach may therefore produce systematically inflated or deflated pooled effects, jeopardizing the validity of hypothesis tests (H1‑H4).
