## Unresolved concerns

- The plan replaces human annotation with a 'simulated annotation engine' generating scores based on known labels (Cut vs. Continuous) plus noise. This creates a circular validation loop: the ground truth is mathematically derived from the stratification label, which is the same variable the divergence metric is hypothesized to predict. This guarantees a high correlation (construct validity failure) and invalidates the hypothesis test, as the 'ground truth' is not an independent measure of semantic discontinuity but a synthetic proxy of the sampling strategy.
- The plan relies on DAVIS and UCF101 labels (e.g., 'Walking', 'Jumping') to define 'Continuous' vs. 'Cut' for the simulation. However, these dataset labels are often coarse (action class) and do not guarantee the presence of 'abrupt scene cuts' within a 16-frame clip. Without independent, frame-level ground truth for cuts (e.g., from a dedicated cut-detection dataset or manual verification), the 'Cut' condition is ill-defined, leading to noise in the independent variable that the simulation cannot correct.
- The power analysis justifies N=500 for detecting r ≈ 0.12. However, the plan uses a 50/50 stratified sample (250 cuts, 250 continuous). In natural video, cuts are rare events. A 50/50 split creates a massive selection bias. While the plan mentions Inverse-Probability Weighting (IPW), IPW corrects for prevalence in the *population*, not for the *definition* of the ground truth. If the 'Cut' label is noisy (see previous concern), IPW will not recover the true correlation, and the power calculation for r=0.12 becomes invalid for the actual population of interest.
- The plan rejects 'Real human annotation' as impossible in CI and opts for simulation. This is a methodological failure for a research project claiming to validate a 'semantic' metric. The hypothesis is about *semantic* discontinuity. Simulating semantic scores based on file labels does not test the model's ability to detect semantic discontinuity; it only tests if the model's numerical error correlates with the *dataset's* labeling noise. The methodology cannot answer the research question.
